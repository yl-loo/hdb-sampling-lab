Things to try, in order

  1. Draw one sample. Compare its mean with the population mean. Draw another. Did the number change? That variation is sampling variability.
  2. Click +1,000. The sample flats are right-skewed like the population, but the sample means pile up in a symmetric bell that follows the t-distribution curve.
  3. Click Pin for comparison, change n to 100, then draw 1,000 more. Ten times fewer flats per sample gives a spread about √10 ≈ 3.2 times wider.
  4. Switch to Convenience sample and draw again. The bell is just as narrow but centred in the wrong place. A bigger n cannot fix bias.

Check your understanding

Is the sampling distribution a distribution of flats?

No. Each dot in panel 3 is one whole sample of n flats boiled down to a single number. Panel 2 shows flats; panel 3 shows statistics.

Why is panel 3 so much narrower than panel 1?

Averaging cancels out unusually cheap and unusually expensive flats. So the means of large samples stay much closer together than individual flat prices do.

Does a larger sample size fix a convenience sample?

No. A larger n reduces variability (spread) but leaves bias (the off-centre shift) untouched. Random selection removes bias; sample size controls precision.

    Think about it

    Does your interval definitely contain μ?

    No. Your interval either contains μ or it does not. The 95% describes the method: if every student in the class drew a sample and built an interval this way, about 95% of the intervals would contain μ.

    Why use s and t* instead of σ and 1.96?

    In real life we do not know the population SD σ, so we estimate it with the sample SD s. That extra uncertainty is why we use the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom. For large n, t* is very close to 1.96.

    What happens to the interval if n is 100 instead of 1,000?

    The standard error s/√n gets about √10 ≈ 3.2 times bigger. The critical value t* also changes: with df = 99 it is 1.984 instead of 1.962 (df = 999), because a smaller sample gives a less reliable estimate of the spread. Both changes widen the interval, so it ends up about 3.2 times wider. Smaller samples give less precise estimates.

    Compare with a classmate. Why are your intervals different?

    You drew different random samples, so you got different values of x̄ and s. The intervals move around from sample to sample, while μ stays fixed.

      Think about it

      What would "independent" look like in the mosaic plot?

      Both columns would split at the same height, like the expected mosaic, and both floor-level rows in the table would show the same share of million-dollar flats as the Total row. Knowing a flat's floor level would tell you nothing extra about its price group.

      Where do the expected counts come from?

      They are the counts we would expect if H₀ were true: the overall share in each price group applied to each floor level. That works out to row total × column total ÷ grand total.

      Try n = 200 a few times. What goes wrong?

      Only about 5 high-floor million-dollar flats are expected, and in many samples the smallest expected count drops below 5. The chi-square approximation is not reliable when expected counts are that small, so we need a bigger sample.

      Does a significant result prove high floors cause higher prices?

      No. The test shows an association. Other variables could explain it: high-floor flats tend to be newer, larger, or in more central towns. Association is not causation.

      Why does the test on all 25,085 flats give such a huge χ²?

      χ² grows with sample size for the same pattern. With the whole population there is no sampling uncertainty to test; the table itself tells us the association exists. Tests are for drawing conclusions about a population from a sample.
